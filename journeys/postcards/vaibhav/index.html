A Postcard from Vaibhav JECRC Jaipur → Future First → Family business reinventor

Hi,

Let me start with something honest.

I'm not writing to you from an IIT. I'm not writing from an IIM. I'm not writing from a fancy startup office in Bangalore.

I'm writing from my family business — a business I once thought I would never join, in a city that's not in any LinkedIn highlight reel, doing work that nobody told me would make me successful.

And yet — I am. Really, genuinely successful. By the only definition that actually matters: I love what I do, I'm growing it bigger every year, and I built it on my own terms.

Let me tell you how I got here.

1. Engineering at JECRC, Jaipur. The kind of college most middle-class Indian students actually go to. In college I discovered I genuinely loved math — not memorising it, but playing with numbers, finding patterns, enjoying the puzzle. I didn't know yet what I'd do with it. But I followed it.
2. Joining Future First (the "dream job"). A financial services firm that hires people who love math, logic, problem-solving — the kind of place most tier-1 engineering grads chase. Good salary, impressive brand. On paper my life looked sorted. But something kept asking me: Yeh meri zindagi hai? Is this what I want to do for the next 30 years?
3. The hardest decision. I quit. Without another job offer. Without a backup plan. Parents worried. Relatives asked questions. Friends thought I'd lost my mind. But I knew I wanted to build something — not be one more employee in a system.
4. Coming back to the family business. In our culture, joining a family business is often seen as "failure ka backup plan." That's a huge mistake. I didn't join it like a tired son taking over his father's work — I joined it like a founder taking over a company. I brought everything I'd learned: math, finance, strategy, data, technology. I modernised operations. Built systems where there were none. Used data to make better decisions. Expanded into new areas. Revenue doubled. Then tripled. The business is bigger, stronger, more profitable than it has ever been.

I make my own decisions. I work on my own ideas. I take risks. I learn. I build. I'm an entrepreneur in every sense — except the business has been in my family for years, instead of years to come.

Don't be embarrassed about your family business. Be excited. There is a startup hidden in it, waiting for you to find it.
— Vaibhav · JECRC Jaipur · Family business reinventor
If this road feels right

Here's the honest map — for students who won't (or don't want to) go to IIT or IIM

Most career advice is written for IIT/IIM students. This one is for everyone else.

Now → Class 11–12

Pick the stream you can actually do well in

If you love numbers, PCM with Math is great. If you love business or your family runs one, Commerce is genuinely useful. Pick what you'll work hard at, not what sounds impressive.

And start observing the businesses around you — your family's shop, your neighbour's factory, the kirana store. How they price. How they manage stock. Why customers come back. This is the most underrated education.

Class 12 → College

Aim for the best college you can — but don't get stuck if it's not IIT/NIT

  • If you crack JEE / NEET — wonderful, go.
  • If you don't — that is completely fine. Plenty of great careers start from JECRC, VIT, SRM, Manipal, Amity, top state colleges and good private universities.
  • Commerce / BBA route — Christ University, NMIMS, Symbiosis, IIM IPM programmes, top DU colleges
  • The trick is hunger — pick the best college you can, walk in like you have something to prove, and learn more than your degree teaches.
During college

Build skills your degree doesn't teach

  • One real skill, deeply — Excel + financial modelling, or coding, or design
  • Internships in small companies — you'll learn 10× more than at a brand-name firm
  • If your family runs a business — spend summers actually working in it. Watch. Ask questions. Don't just sit in the AC office.
  • Read business stories — ‘Stay Hungry Stay Foolish’, ‘Built from Scratch’, ‘The Tata Group’ — Indian entrepreneurs you can actually relate to.
First job (age 22–25)

Get exposure to how real businesses work

Whether you land at a financial firm, a startup, or a tier-2 IT company — your real goal is the same: understand how money is made, how customers are won, how operations actually run. You're not collecting a brand name; you're stealing a business education someone else is paying you for.

Two to four years is usually enough. Stay longer only if you're still learning.

The pivot point (age 25–28)

Listen when something inside you keeps asking "is this it?"

This is the hardest stage. Salary is good. Society approves. Everyone tells you not to change anything. But if a quieter voice keeps asking "yeh meri zindagi hai?" — that voice is data.

You can pivot to: your family business, a second-time startup job closer to operating roles, an MBA, or your own company. None of these is failure. All of them require courage. Don't take the leap blindly — but don't ignore the voice either.

Joining the family business

Treat it like a startup — because it is one

  • Modernise operations — accounting software, inventory systems, digital invoicing
  • Build a data muscle — what's the real margin per product / per customer? Most family businesses don't know.
  • Find one growth lever — online sales, a new product line, a new geography, a new customer segment
  • Hire 1–2 ambitious people younger than you — that's how a small business becomes a real company
  • Respect what works — the trust your parents built with customers and suppliers is more valuable than any MBA
Your college doesn't decide your finishing line. Pivots are not detours — they are the path. And your family business isn't a fallback — it's a startup with a 20-year head start. — Vaibhav
